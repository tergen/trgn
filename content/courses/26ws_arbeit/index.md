## Course Information

**Instructor:** Dr. Timur Ergen (iawlehre@uni-bremen.de)  
**Institution:** University of Bremen, Institute Labor and Economy (*iaw*)  
**Course Code:** 08-SOZ-BA-2-Soz-SP-3  
**Language:** German  
**Semester:** Winter 2026/27  
**Schedule:** Mondays 14:00–16:00, weekly (from 19/10/26)  
**Location:** UNICOM 3.3390

The course introduces the sociology of work. It covers theoretical foundations, central dimensions of paid work and working life, and current controversies about the changing world of work. Its guiding question is how modern societies organize, distribute, and regulate work, and how the relationship between work, associations, market, and state has shifted historically.

The course is organized in three parts. A first part covers classical and more recent theoretical foundations of the field: the division of labor and productivity; exploitation and alienation; the ethic of the calling; the commodification of labor as a 'fictitious commodity'; control and deskilling in the labor process; and emotional labor. A second part addresses the institutional embedding and social constitution of work in economy and society, from occupations, skills, and professions through labor markets and forms of work organization to the role of trade unions and collective interest representation and the relationship between paid work and the welfare state. The final part turns to current debates on the changing world of work: migrant labor, platform work, and the institutional dualization of labor market regimes and working conditions.

Besides providing an overview of key concepts and findings of the sociology of work, the course familiarizes students with the variety of sociological approaches to work, ranging from the classics of social theory through comparative institutional analyses to ethnographic and quantitative studies. Where possible, topics are related to the German case, for instance codetermination and free collective bargaining, the dual vocational training system, or the expansion of the low-wage sector.

## Assessment

Discussion of the readings is the core of the seminar. If you do not plan to read the texts and come to the sessions prepared, you will get extremely little out of the seminar – next to nothing, in fact. All students are therefore expected to read all assigned texts and to participate regularly and actively. To get credit, students must (1) read all assigned texts, (2) take an active part in the seminar discussions, and (3) pass an oral examination at the end of the semester.

## Offline Commitment

The seminar is designed as a social offline space. We work with printed texts, and with pens and paper. By common agreement, laptops, tablets and phones stay unused during our sessions. We also do without language models, whether for summaries, for preparation, or for written work. If you print the ca. 440 pages we read in the seminar at the SUUB, the full reader will amount to around €22. The offline commitment has nothing to do with hostility to technology. At its core, the craft of the social sciences consists of reading texts, working your way through them, and writing texts of your own: following an argument, systematically questioning it, and developing your own arguments. This ability only comes with practice, and it is increasingly under pressure because summarizing, phrasing and structuring have become so easy to outsource. Whatever you outsource, you will not learn. The effort of reading a difficult text multiple times, or of rewriting a clumsy paragraph again and again, is not a detour on the way to a clear thought. It is an important form in which thinking itself takes place, and it lies at the center of studying the social sciences at German universities.
