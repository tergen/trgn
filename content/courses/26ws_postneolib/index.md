**Location:** UNICOM 3.0210

The course examines reconfigurations of economic policy in the 21st century with an emphasis on the evolution of Europe in the international political economy. The guiding thread of the course is the question of the resilience of neoliberalism as an economic policy paradigm since the early 2000s. A major feature of the class is to bring into common view multiple key fields of contemporary economic policy-making – such as competition, environmental, fiscal, labour, industrial, monetary, regulatory, and security policies. The course is organised in three major parts. In a first part, the course explores foundational debates on neoliberalism, its global regime features, and international varieties. A second part introduces five key arenas of cutting-edge analyses of recent economic policy experimentation marking a shift away from the neoliberal script: state responses to emergencies and crises, the return of infrastructures as public policy concerns, the greening of economic policies, the revived interest in industrial and innovation policies, and the geo-politicisation of economic policy. The last third of the course explores major political economic takes on how to think about an emerging 'postneoliberal' order.

Besides introducing students to cutting-edge debates in political economy in multiple fields, the class deploys the problem of emerging postneoliberal orders to develop a range of foundational ideas in comparative, historical, and international public policy analysis and political economy. Key debates touched upon concern problems of business power, concepts of institutional and policy change, the evolution of democratic capitalism, European integration, institutional constraints on policy-making, problems of state capacity and bureaucratic competences, multilevel politics and policy implementation, policy instrument design, and varieties of growth regimes. Methodologically, students will be acquainted with a broad range of contemporary research designs in comparative and international political economy. As a research-led course featuring multi-disciplinary high-level publications, the class furthermore helps to familiarize students with techniques to craft and situate scholarly arguments in fields of ongoing scholarly debate, ranging from data-driven, historical, and comparative designs through to policy-oriented arguments connecting to current public policy debates.

## Assessment

Students are required to prepare one brief presentation and submit a final term paper. The presentation builds on the session on varieties of neoliberalism. In sessions 4 through 12, students will present brief analyses of national political economies of their choice, with an emphasis on diagnoses of the given country's economic policy trajectories under neoliberalism (10 minutes each, in groups of two). Emphasis of the presentations must be on the given country's institutional idiosyncrasies, political economic structure, and historical evolution. Detailed instructions and the allocation of countries will be discussed in session 3, as will deadlines, subjects, structure, and requirements for the final paper.

## Offline Commitment

This class will commit itself to being an offline space. We work with printed texts, and with pens and paper. Laptops, tablets and phones stay stashed away during our sessions, and we aim to not use language models, neither for summaries, preparation, or for written work. If you print the ca. 340 pages we read in the seminar at the SUUB, the full reader will amount to around €17. This is not about being against technology. At its core, the craft of a social scientist is reading and working your way through texts and writing texts of your own: following a line of argument, challenging it, and developing your own arguments. This ability only comes with practice, and it is increasingly under pressure because summarizing, phrasing and structuring have become so easy to outsource. Whatever you outsource, you won't learn, making you increasingly dependent on an industry promising to eventually sell you 'intelligence on a meter' to a degree that justifies multi-trillion-dollar valuations. The effort of reading a difficult text multiple times, or of rewriting a clumsy paragraph again and again, is not a detour on the way to a clear thought. It is the very process of thinking we are practising in universities.
