The seminar explores central perspectives on the relationship between economy and society, from the origins of liberal economic thought to more recent theories of democratic capitalism. Seminar readings reflect the major political-economic controversies of modernity: the origin and peculiarities of the capitalist economic system; the role of politics in a liberal economic order; the need for reform and the possibility of revolution; the limits of state regulation and social control of the capitalist economy; and the relationship between the capitalist market economy and modern culture. The class has been conceptualized by the directors of the Max Planck Institute for the Study of Societies as part of the core curriculum of the International Max Planck Research School on the Social and Political Constitution of the Economy.

## Assessment

Discussion of the readings will make up the core of the seminar sessions. Students are expected to read all assigned texts and to participate regularly and actively. To get credit (6 CP), students must (1) read all assigned texts; (2) prepare three reading reports (4,000 characters each); and (3) submit a term paper (12,000 characters).

The reading reports are meant to prepare the ground for discussion by asking participants to lay out their reactions to the readings in written form. The reports should not simply summarize the readings but rather pick up on specific arguments, compare the positions of different authors, raise questions of evidence, usefulness, or plausibility, or discuss particular strengths and weaknesses in the arguments and descriptions. We will circulate these reports via Stud.IP. To allow everyone time to read each other's comments, they must be submitted by 4 pm the day before the respective session.

## Offline Commitment

This class will commit itself to being an offline space. We work with printed texts, and with pens and paper. Laptops, tablets and phones stay stashed away during our sessions, and we aim to not use language models, neither for summaries, preparation, or for written work. If you print the ca. 400 pages we read in the seminar at the SUUB, the full reader will amount to around €20. This is not about being against technology. At its core, the craft of a social scientist is reading and working your way through texts and writing texts of your own: following a line of argument, challenging it, and developing your own arguments. This ability only comes with practice, and it is increasingly under pressure because summarizing, phrasing and structuring have become so easy to outsource. Whatever you outsource, you won't learn, making you increasingly dependent on an industry promising to eventually sell you 'intelligence on a meter' to a degree that justifies multi-trillion-dollar valuations. The effort of reading a difficult text multiple times, or of rewriting a clumsy paragraph again and again, is not a detour on the way to a clear thought. It is the very process of thinking we are practising in universities.
